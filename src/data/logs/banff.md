### Day 2: Lake Louise
Lake Louise is _really_ **Blue**. The type of blue you'd think all bodies of water look like as a kid. The type of blue you'd want to drink to quench your 3AM thirst. Honestly, there should be a color to describe the specifc blue.

![Lake Louise](/images/logs/banff/DSCF0308.webp)

#### **Why is Lake Louise blue?**
Short tangent but I found it interesting to learn that the blue color these lakes get are from a phenomenon that occurs when there's a glacier that _feeds_ into the lake. Essentially, the glacier grinds against the mountain it's on which creates this ***Rock Flour***. Once these extremely fine particles of rock enter the lake, they stay suspended instead of sinking due to their tiny size. Once these particles are hit with sunlight, they scatter the blue and green wavelengths of light, creating **Lake Louise Blue**. 
###

Originally we simply wanted to see Lake Louise, maybe cycle around, then head on to another area (possibly Banff city). We reach Louise and it starts to rain. We want to make the most of this stop so we start our walk around the lake. Once we reach the end, we see a board indicating that there's a hike that goes behind Lake Louise named [The Plain of Six Glaciers](https://www.alltrails.com/trail/canada/alberta/plain-of-six-glaciers-trail). Now I heard that cool ass name and new we had to give it a shot. Despite the rain, almost single-digit temperature, and little to no gear, we gave it a shot.

After a grueling but stunning 3 hour hike through gravel and occasional snow to the peak, we finally made it. There was a cool shack at the top selling soup, tea, coffee, P&B sandwiches, etc. Not sure who's idea that was but they're geniuses - we were all starving by then.

### Day 3: Class 4+ White Water Rafting

![Before](/images/logs/banff/rafting-start.webp)

I always considered myself a good swimmer and not one to be scared of water but this honestly got me. Our instructor **Dean** (rightmost in the picture) was quite a character. He talked about how this is his first time rafting (joke of course). He'd been rafting for 22 years, that too all over the globe from India to South Africa. Throughout the session he'd yell "Yeehaw", never thought I'd hear that unironically and think it was cool... 

Dean had 1 and only 1 rule for us: listen and apply all commands that come out of his mouth. The main commands were:
- Forward + Backward
- Left + Right
    - all 4 of these had a **"HARD!"** alternate which indicated extreme danger
- Hold on
    - this indicated stopping paddling and gripping the rope that covered the entire middle of the raft
- Get Down
    - this was the scary one. it meant we stop paddling, jump in the boat and hold on for dear life. issue was that he'd need us back on the paddle within 1-2 seconds, its harder than it sounds in a slippery boat with a person right next to you

Not to flex or anything but Dean had me in the front of the raft even though he knew I hadn't ever rafted before. His mentality was that he needed someone "strong" in the front so he chose me. Little did he know...

###

Rafting comes in 4 (legal) classes of technical difficulty:

- Class I: Easy
    - fast moving water but no obstructions 
    - basic paddling
    - low risk
- Class II: Novice
    - entering rapids but wide and clear; medium-sized waves
    - basic maneuvering
    - still low risk
- Class III: Intermediate
    - rapids with irregular waves capable of swampning an open canoe
    - good paddle control & ability to follow commmands quick!
- Class IV: Advanced
    -  intense rapids with large waves capable of capsizing a raft
    - precise boat control, strong physical fitness, and mandatory rescue skills

We knew that this river was a Class IV at its worst but Dean informed us that thanks to the rain the day before, we'd be in something called a **Class IV+**. 
###
Honestly, the experience was something I'd recommend to everyone, no matter the age or fitness level. Rafting is a team sport which requires the whole raft as a whole to coordinate as one unit. Dean is only as good as his team and we are only good if we listen which we did.

Towards the end of the session, there came a reasonably steady part of the river. Dean told us that if we were thinking of getting in the river and getting a feel of it, it's now or never. My ego was inflated with my great swimming skills from the past so I took him up on that along with 2 other people. The moment I got in, I felt my feet sweep under the raft, almost as if I was about to get sucked under it. The water was unimaginably quick and fierce. 

Within a minute, I decided enough was enough and that it was time to get out. On my first attempt, I was all the way up but the damn raft had nothing to grab on to on the inside so I fell back in. I tried 2-3 more times after which my legs gave out and I could not push anymore. Luckily, the guy sitting opposite to me was kind enough to lift me out of the water. That experience was pretty awakening.

#### **The Crossing**
![The Crossing](/images/logs/banff/DSCF0395.webp)
The Crossing is a resort that comes roughly halfway through the distance between Banff to Jasper. This is the only place you can access signals/WiFi on the entire highway. We decided to take a detour from there which, if we followed, could have taken us to Red Deer and Edmonton. The reason for this was that my parents stayed a night at Nordegg which was en route. They mentioned that this road was beautiful so we decided to take the 30 minute detour despite a 5 hour total drive. 

![Abraham Lake Enroute to Nordegg](/images/logs/banff/DSCF0488.webp)
Words can't quite explain what I felt standing here. The entire route to this random RV lot had no other car but ours. We saw deer and mountain goats but not a single other human. That same breeze I felt at Peyto Lake was back. I wanted to lay there forever and just be.
